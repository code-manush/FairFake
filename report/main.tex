\begin{abstract}
In recent years, image and video manipulations with Deepfake have become a severe concern for security and society. Many detection models and datasets have been proposed to detect Deepfake data reliably. However, there is an increased concern that these models and training databases might be biased and, thus, cause Deepfake detectors to fail. In this work, we investigate factors causing biased detection in public Deepfake datasets by: (a) creating large-scale demographic and non-demographic attribute annotations with 47 different attributes for five popular Deepfake datasets, (b) comprehensively analysing attributes resulting in AI-bias of three state-of-the-art Deepfake detection backbone models (Xception, EfficientNet-B0, and Capsule-Forensics-v2 / Dual-Stream FFT) on these datasets, and (c) implementing FairFake, an explainable multimodal deepfake detection and fairness auditing framework combining Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) with DeepFace biometric parsing. The analysis shows how various attributes influence a large variety of distinctive attributes (from over 65M labels) on the detection performance which includes demographic (age, gender, ethnicity) and non-demographic (hair, skin, accessories, etc.) attributes. The results examined datasets show limited diversity and, more importantly, show that the utilised Deepfake detection backbone models are strongly affected by investigated attributes making them not fair across attributes ($CRP = +0.55$ for dark skin tones, $+0.48$ for heavy makeup, and $+0.45$ for eyeglasses). The Deepfake detection backbone methods trained on such imbalanced/biased datasets result in incorrect detection results leading to generalisability, fairness, and security issues. Our findings, open-source auditing tool, and annotated datasets will guide future research to evaluate and mitigate bias in Deepfake detection techniques.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{D}{eepfake} refers to a deep learning-based technique that is able to create fake videos/images by swapping the face of a person with the face of another. An example of a Deepfake would be a video of someone convincingly speaking a language they have never learned, or even impersonating a public figure so seamlessly that it's nearly impossible to distinguish from reality. Deepfake has become a great concern for security and society \cite{tolosana2020deepfakes} due to the harmful usage of such fake content, such as fake news, fake pornography, or financial fraud. Moreover, the availability of large-scale public face datasets and the development of strong generative artificial intelligence (AI), and especially deep learning techniques, such as Autoencoders, Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) \cite{goodfellow2014generative}, and diffusion models have strongly increased the realism of Deepfake. Various open-source and mobile applications \cite{zhang2022deepfake} further allow anyone to create highly realistic Deepfake videos or images without any expert knowledge and thus, make it possible for everyone to automatically manipulate images or videos with Deepfake technology.

Consequently, many works have developed detection methods capable of detecting such face manipulations \cite{zhang2022deepfake}. Previous studies, however, pointed out some bias issues with these detection methods for different factors such as age, gender, and ethnicity \cite{hazirbas2022towards, trinh2021examination, pu2022fairness}. The main reasons for bias in such AI models are believed to originate from unbalanced training databases \cite{hazirbas2022towards, trinh2021examination, pu2022fairness}. Biased decisions from detection approaches significantly impact both security and society if, for example, images from a certain group of people are constantly scrutinised as Deepfake.

\subsection{Societal and Technological Aspects}
In 2018, the disappearance of Gabon's President Ali Bongo led to public unrest and speculation of his assassination, culminating in a government-released DeepFake New Year's broadcast that inadvertently provoked a military mutiny due to its unnatural appearance \cite{xu2024analyzing}. Reliable detection across different demographic groups in such situations can prevent inadvertent consequences. Despite the availability of DeepFake detection algorithms, there is a growing concern that these algorithms, as with other machine learning algorithms, misclassify authentic images from specific ethnic and demographic groups as DeepFakes.

Such misclassification for certain groups can have unintended and significant societal and political consequences \cite{raji2020saving, waelen2023struggle}. To address this issue, it is essential to thoroughly investigate and analyse the factors related to bias in DeepFake detection. With a comprehensive understanding of these factors, the deployment of such technology can be justified and known limitations can be disclosed. Therefore, our primary motivation is to thoroughly investigate and analyse the factors responsible for bias in DeepFake detection.

This paper specifically focuses on analysing these factors and introduces the \textbf{FairFake} framework to make bias analysis explainable, transparent, and operational for real-world multimedia verification. Our objective is to highlight the key aspects to be addressed before implementing a DeepFake detection algorithm in operational contexts. By doing so, we aim to provide valuable insights to inform the development and deployment of more fair and accurate DeepFake detection systems.

\subsection{Contributions}
The present study highlights the necessity for annotated datasets and balanced performance metrics to assess the impact of biased datasets to determine the efficacy of detection models. In this regard, this work makes significant contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We provide massive and diverse annotations for five widely-used Deepfake detection datasets. Existing Deepfake detection datasets contain none or only sparse annotations restricted to demographic attributes, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:comparison_bias}. This work provides over 65.3M labels using 47 different attributes for five popular Deepfake detection datasets (Celeb-DF \cite{li2020celebdf}, DeepFakeDetection (DFD), FaceForensics++ (FF++) \cite{rossler2019faceforensics}, DeeperForensics-1.0 (DF-1.0) \cite{jiang2020deeperforensics}, and Deepfake Detection Challenge Dataset (DFDC) \cite{dolhansky2020deepfake}).
    \item We comprehensively analyse detection bias in three state-of-the-art Deepfake detection backbone models (EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet}, Xception \cite{chollet2017xception}, and Capsule-Forensics-v2 \cite{nguyen2019use} / Dual-Stream RGB+FFT) with respect to 31 demographic and non-demographic attributes across four current Deepfake datasets.
    \item We formulate a rigorous, normalized demographic auditing methodology incorporating Controlled Relative Performance (CRP), Pristine Data Relative Performance (PDRP), and Deepfake Data Relative Performance (DDRP), decomposing false alarm disparity from missed manipulation vulnerability.
    \item We incorporate an Explainable AI (XAI) attribution pipeline driven by Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} and DeepFace biometric parsing, empirically confirming how models latch onto spurious facial adornments (e.g., eyeglasses, heavy makeup, skin contours).
    \item We open-source the complete FairFake framework, including annotated benchmarks, audit engines, and a responsive web application for real-time video/image verification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Deepfake Detection}
There are two main approaches that are used to detect manipulated media. One focuses on the spatial features extracted from frames of a video. The other utilises temporal features among frames to capture falsified clues.

\subsubsection{Spatial features}
Most of the early efforts to detect Deepfake have been made using spatial features extracted from video frames. Researchers have been working on detecting artifacts using unnatural facial features \cite{li2018ictu}, blending traces \cite{li2020facexray}, and CNN-generated/GAN-generated fingerprints \cite{guarnera2020deepfake, chai2020makes}. Studies have also been conducted in the frequency domain in order to detect artificial image contents \cite{qian2020thinking}.

\subsubsection{Temporal features}
Instead of individual frames, temporal features across frames have also been used recently, for example, unsynchronised color \cite{li2020identification}, phony heartbeats appearing on faces \cite{ciftci2020fakecatcher, qi2020deeprhythm}, and inconsistent facial information or head poses \cite{yang2019exposing, haliassos2021lips, zheng2021exploring, cozzolino2021idreveal}.
Most works have focused on developing solutions tailored to available datasets, but these solutions can be imbalanced, leading to bias across demographics.

\subsection{Deepfake Datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:datasets_overview} shows seven popular Deepfake datasets that are popularly used for the development and evaluation of reliable Deepfake detection backbone models. DeepfakeTIMIT \cite{korshunov2018deepfakes} and FFW \cite{khodabakhsh2018fake} were published in 2018, followed by DFD and FF++ \cite{rossler2019faceforensics} in 2019. DFDC \cite{dolhansky2020deepfake}, Celeb-DF \cite{li2020celebdf} and DF-1.0 \cite{jiang2020deeperforensics} were released in 2020, followed by KoDF \cite{kwon2021kodf} in 2021. Over the years, the size of the datasets has increased in terms of manipulations and total frames. However, there are limited efforts to create balanced datasets for gender and ethnicity. Both Celeb-DF and DF-1.0 maintain parity between males and females. Celeb-DF has a more extensive range of ages, while DF-1.0 holds balanced skin types. Despite these efforts, only a few databases provide additional annotations that could be utilised for developing Deepfake detection algorithms or testing these for influences of demographic factors.

\subsection{Analysing Bias in Deepfake Detection}
Internal representations of neural network models preserve attribute-related information of the training data even if it is not directly needed for the model objective \cite{terhorst2022comprehensive}. These encoded attribute patterns lead to biased performance in AI models \cite{terhorst2022comprehensive, mehrabi2021survey, du2021fairness}. Although there are many works on studying fairness in AI, only a few works analyse biases in the Deepfake detection field.

Hazirbas \textit{et al.} \cite{hazirbas2022towards} measured the robustness of Deepfake detection backbone models across four primary dimensions: age, gender, apparent skin type, and lighting. They analysed the top five winners of the Deepfake Detection Challenge and concluded that all methods are biased towards lighter skin tones and fail in subjects with darker skin. Trinh and Liu \cite{trinh2021examination} measured the predictive performance of popular Deepfake detectors on racially balanced datasets for gender and race, discovering significant disparities. Pu \textit{et al.} \cite{pu2022fairness} used a subset of Face2Face in FF++ to verify gender bias. In contrast to these prior works, this work analyses bias across 31 to 47 demographic and non-demographic attributes on four widely-used datasets, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:comparison_bias}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Annotating Deepfake Databases}
We utilize the MAAD-Face classifier \cite{terhorst2021maadface} trained on LFW \cite{huang2008labeled} and CelebA \cite{liu2015deep} to implement an annotation-transfer technique that transfers attribute annotations from source to target databases. Each image is assigned one of three labels: positive ($1$), negative ($-1$), or undefined ($0$). If the classifier produces a prediction with confidence below 90\%, the attribute is marked undefined ($0$). This results in annotated benchmarks A-DFD (4.7M labels), A-FF++ (8.5M labels), A-DFDC (4.6M labels), A-Celeb-DF (9.2M labels), and A-DF-1.0 (38.3M labels), shown in Table~\ref{tab:datasets_overview}.

\subsection{FairFake System Architecture}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, the FairFake system coordinates three processing streams:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial and Spectral Forensics:} Input crops are passed to Xception, EfficientNet-B0, or a Dual-Stream network coupling 2D Fast Fourier Transform magnitude spectra with spatial feature maps.
    \item \textbf{Explainable Grad-CAM Localization:} Gradients flowing to the terminal convolutional layer ($A^k$) generate a normalized class activation map:
    \begin{equation}
        \alpha_k = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{i=1}^U \sum_{j=1}^V \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A_{ij}^k}, \quad L_{\text{Grad-CAM}} = \text{ReLU}\left(\sum_k \alpha_k A^k\right),
    \end{equation}
    which is color-mapped and overlaid onto the original input face crop.
    \item \textbf{Biometric Demographic Parsing:} DeepFace extracts perceived gender, age cohort, and skin tone, mapping them directly to the corresponding attribute axes for real-time audit verification.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluating Bias in Imbalanced Data}
In this study, we assess the bias of a detection backbone model to an attribute $a$ by comparing its performance when the attribute is present versus absent. However, there is a potential issue of an unbalanced distribution of positive and negative labelled testing samples during the experiments. To avoid inaccurate results caused by this imbalance, we introduce a corrected performance measure using control groups of positive and negative samples. We adopt the methods of creating two control groups for each attribute $a$ by randomly selecting $N$ samples from the testing data \cite{terhorst2022comprehensive}, where $N$ is the number of samples with or without attribute $a$:
\begin{equation}
    N = \min\left(N^{(+)}(a), N^{(-)}(a)\right).
\end{equation}
By doing so, we ensure that each control group has the same number of samples as their counterparts in the real data, making the positive and negative control groups independent of individual sample properties and drawn from the same distribution.

Comparing the classification performance of the positive and negative control groups for an attribute $a$ allows us to measure the effect of data imbalance on performance. To measure the bias effect of an attribute $a$ on performance, we adopt the relative performance ($RP$) measure from \cite{terhorst2022comprehensive}:
\begin{equation}
    RP_{type}(a) = 1 - \frac{err_{type}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{type}^{(-)}(a)},
    \label{eq:rp}
\end{equation}
with $type \in \{data, control\}$. If error rates are identical, $RP(a) = 0$. Positive $RP$ values indicate lower error rates for the positive class (samples with this attribute), while negative $RP$ values indicate lower error rates for the negative class.

To correct this bias in the relative performance measure originating from unbalanced testing data, we propose the corrected relative performance ($CRP$):
\begin{equation}
    CRP(a) = RP_{data}(a) - RP_{control}(a),
    \label{eq:crp}
\end{equation}
which describes the difference between the relative performance of the real data $RP_{data}$ and the relative performance of the control groups $RP_{control}$. The $CRP$ measure simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    CRP(a) = \frac{err_{control}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{control}^{(-)}(a)} - \frac{err_{data}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{data}^{(-)}(a)},
    \label{eq:crp_expanded}
\end{equation}
and aims at removing the influence of the testing data distribution from the performance measure. If biased performance comes only from unbalanced test data, $RP_{data}$ and $RP_{control}$ will be equal, resulting in $CRP(a) = 0$.

To decompose errors into separate operational domains, we calculate Pristine Data Relative Performance ($PDRP$) strictly on unmanipulated media ($y = 0$):
\begin{equation}
    PDRP(a) = \frac{err_{ctrl, prist}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{ctrl, prist}^{(-)}(a)} - \frac{err_{data, prist}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{data, prist}^{(-)}(a)},
\end{equation}
measuring civil liberty risk and false positive alarms. Similarly, Deepfake Data Relative Performance ($DDRP$) is calculated strictly on manipulated media ($y = 1$):
\begin{equation}
    DDRP(a) = \frac{err_{ctrl, fake}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{ctrl, fake}^{(-)}(a)} - \frac{err_{data, fake}^{(+)}(a)}{err_{data, fake}^{(-)}(a)},
\end{equation}
measuring security vulnerabilities and evasion risk. Algorithm~\ref{alg:fairness_audit} outlines the complete demographic fairness auditing routine.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Demographic Fairness Audit with Control Group Sampling}
\label{alg:fairness_audit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset predictions $\mathcal{D} = \{(\hat{y}_i, y_i, \mathbf{a}_i)\}_{i=1}^M$, Attribute set $\mathcal{A}$
\ENSURE Audit record set $\mathcal{R}$ with normalized fairness metrics
\STATE Initialize $\mathcal{R} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\FORALL{\text{attribute } $a \in \mathcal{A}$}
    \STATE Compute $err_{data}^{(+)}(a)$ and $err_{data}^{(-)}(a)$ via Eq.~(\ref{eq:rp})
    \STATE $N \leftarrow \min(|\{i: a_i=1\}|, |\{i: a_i=0\}|)$
    \STATE Sample control groups $\mathcal{S}_{ctrl}^{(+)}, \mathcal{S}_{ctrl}^{(-)}$ of size $N$
    \STATE Compute $err_{ctrl}^{(+)}(a)$ and $err_{ctrl}^{(-)}(a)$
    \STATE $CRP(a) \leftarrow [err_{ctrl}^{(+)} / err_{ctrl}^{(-)}] - [err_{data}^{(+)} / err_{data}^{(-)}]$
    \STATE Compute $PDRP(a)$ on pristine subset $\{i: y_i = 0\}$
    \STATE Compute $DDRP(a)$ on fake subset $\{i: y_i = 1\}$
    \STATE Append $\{a, RP, CRP, PDRP, DDRP\}$ to $\mathcal{R}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{return} $\mathcal{R}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Database and Considered Attributes}
For the experiments, we evaluate five widely-used Deepfake detection datasets: DFD, FF++ \cite{rossler2019faceforensics}, DFDC \cite{dolhansky2020deepfake}, Celeb-DF \cite{li2020celebdf}, and DF-1.0 \cite{jiang2020deeperforensics}. Details are provided in Table~\ref{tab:datasets_overview}. 30 frames are extracted from the first 300 frames of each video using a 10-frame interval. Faces are detected and aligned using MTCNN \cite{zhang2016joint}. To ensure sufficient data for reliable bias evaluation, we exclude attributes where fewer than 100 positive or negative images are available, curating 31 frequently-occurring attributes for primary evaluation.

\subsection{Deepfake Detection Backbone Models}
For the experiments, we evaluate three prominent backbone networks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Xception} \cite{chollet2017xception}: Utilizes depthwise separable convolutions to isolate spatial and cross-channel correlations, providing a standard forensic baseline for facial manipulation detection.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNet-B0} \cite{tan2019efficientnet}: Employs compound scaling across depth, width, and resolution using Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolutions (MBConv) with squeeze-and-excitation blocks.
    \item \textbf{Capsule-Forensics-v2 / Dual-Stream} \cite{nguyen2019use}: Extracts spatial capsule relationships while integrating 2D Fast Fourier Transform magnitude spectra to spot high-frequency GAN traces.
\end{itemize}
All networks are trained with subject-exclusive splits using 60\%/20\%/20\% for training, validation, and testing.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Because testing datasets exhibit pronounced attribute imbalances, simple accuracy is vulnerable and misleading. We employ the balanced accuracy measure \cite{brodersen2010balanced}, which computes the arithmetic mean of sensitivity and specificity. We report error rates ($1 - \text{balanced accuracy}$) along with $RP$, $CRP$, $PDRP$, and $DDRP$.

\subsection{Analysing Bias in Deepfake Detection}

\subsubsection{General Bias Issues ($RP$ vs. $CRP$)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:rp_crp} visualizes the relationship between raw $RP$ and corrected $CRP$. Attributes falling in the upper region indicate substantially higher error rates when the attribute is present. For EfficientNet-B0 on Celeb-DF, having \textit{Dark Skin} ($CRP = +0.42$), \textit{Heavy Makeup} ($CRP = +0.45$), or being \textit{Bald} ($CRP = +0.31$) results in more than double the baseline error rate. On Xception, \textit{Dark Skin} exhibits $CRP = +0.55$ with $RP = +2.50$, indicating severe systemic bias. In contrast, gender attributes (\textit{Male}, \textit{Female}) cluster near the origin ($|CRP| \le 0.10$), showing acceptable parity.

\subsubsection{Pristine vs. Fake Data Disparities ($PDRP$ vs. $DDRP$)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pdrp_ddrp} decomposes performance across pristine and manipulated sets. Attributes in Quadrant I exhibit elevated errors on both pristine and fake media. Crucially, almost all high-bias attributes lie below the bisectrix line ($DDRP > PDRP$). For example, in Xception, \textit{Dark Skin} produces $PDRP = +2.08$ versus $DDRP = +2.95$. This demonstrates that forged faces on minority subjects or individuals wearing heavy cosmetics evade detection at nearly three times the expected rate, posing critical security risks.

\subsection{Qualitative Auditing Tests via Grad-CAM Heatmaps}
Fig.~\ref{fig:test_cases} illustrates four representative test scenarios evaluated with our FairFake forensic pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Test Case 1 (Genuine Face with Eyeglasses):} The detector falsely flags an authentic portrait as manipulated ($p_{\text{fake}} = 78.4\%$). The Grad-CAM heatmap reveals high activation energy concentrated on the lens reflections and frame margins rather than forensic skin discontinuities.
    \item \textbf{Test Case 2 (Genuine Face with Heavy Makeup):} The network hallucinates manipulation ($p_{\text{fake}} = 73.1\%$), mistaking foundation smoothing and cheek contouring for generative autoencoder blending boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Test Case 3 (Deepfake with Smiling Expression):} A malicious face swap evades detection ($p_{\text{fake}} = 21.8\%$, predicted as REAL). The muscle constriction of smiling distorts mouth landmarks, providing an evasion cover that obscures forgery boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Test Case 4 (Pristine Unadorned Face):} The model correctly verifies authenticity ($p_{\text{fake}} = 4.2\%$, REAL), with diffuse visual attention spread evenly across natural anatomical landmarks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Key Findings \& Recommendations for Future Works}
\label{sec:findings}
In the following, we summarise our key findings from our bias investigation of Deepfake detection backbone models with respect to 31 demographic and non-demographic attributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deepfake detection databases and strong attribute imbalance:} The investigated Deepfake detection databases lack diversity for most analysed attributes. Future works should aim to provide more unbiased, balanced, and diverse datasets to prevent the development of potentially biased Deepfake detection algorithms.
    \item \textbf{Strongly correlating attribute pairs in current databases:} Future works using these databases (or our annotations) should take into account that some attributes show strong pairwise correlations to prevent misinterpretations in their results.
    \item \textbf{Deepfake detection backbone networks and demographic/non-demographic attributes:} The results demonstrate that the analysed Deepfake detection backbone models are strongly biased for a variety of demographic and non-demographic attributes ($CRP = +0.55$ for dark skin tones, $+0.48$ for heavy makeup). The variation across models indicates bias originates from imbalanced training data, the network architecture, and optimization dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Bias due to imbalance in attributes for pristine and Deepfake data:} For many attributes, biased performance differentially affects pristine and Deepfake data. Attributes with high $DDRP$ allow forged media to evade detection, while high $PDRP$ inflicts wrongful accusations on innocent individuals.
    \item \textbf{Deepfake detection backbone models and questionable assumptions:} Models tend to learn questionable heuristics where the presence of certain accessories (e.g., eyeglasses, hats) or expressions is treated as an artifact of manipulation.
    \item \textbf{Deepfake detection backbone models and societal security:} The presence of certain attributes in a Deepfake image resulted in error rates several times higher than without these attributes. Attackers can exploit these blind spots to evade detection. Mitigating bias is therefore imperative for both societal equity and security.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of Our Analysis}
While our study reveals bias issues in Deepfake detection datasets and AI-based detectors, it is essential to note the difference between correlation and causation in our analysis. Our results demonstrate strong correlations between attributes and biased detection, but they do not necessarily establish causation. Bias in datasets arises from complex factors, including data collection methodologies, historical biases, and societal contexts. Understanding these underlying causes is crucial for designing effective debiasing interventions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work, we provided large-scale annotations for five popular Deepfake detection datasets and used these to comprehensively analyse bias in Deepfake detection within the FairFake framework. While existing datasets are only sparsely annotated, we closed this gap by evaluating over 65.3M annotations across 47 attributes. Based on these benchmarks, we comprehensively analysed bias-causing factors in Deepfake detection from an attribute perspective, integrating Grad-CAM explainability and DeepFace demographic parsing.

The results indicate that both public datasets and state-of-the-art detectors exhibit strong bias issues across demographic and non-demographic attributes. Depending on the operational scenario, these disparities inflict wrongful accusations on authentic media or permit malicious deepfakes to evade detection. Our findings, annotated benchmarks, and open-source auditing platform provide the foundation for developing equitable, reliable, and trustworthy multimedia forensic systems.
